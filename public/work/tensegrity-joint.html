Tensegrity-Based Variable-Stiffness Actuator for a Knee Exoskeleton

NITK Mechanical Engineering major project · May 2026 — Present

Under the guidance of Prof. S. M. Kulkarni.

We wanted to make a wearable knee joint that could flex under a sudden load and provide support when needed. Our design uses tendon tension to explore how the joint’s stiffness could be adjusted. Twelve support tendons suspend a three-armed piece between the upper and lower frames.

We modelled walking, running, and squatting in Simulink, then checked how the support tendons and rigid parts carried loads in separate sizing analyses.

Why use tendons?

Walking, running, and squatting ask different things of a knee joint. Running applies a load quickly, while a squat loads the knee through a slower change in posture. We wanted the same joint to allow some movement under a sudden load and offer more support when needed.

The tendons are pulled tight before the joint carries any external load. That initial tension is a way to change how the joint responds. It also helps keep the network stable as some tendons take more load and others lose tension. Since a tendon can only pull, a span that loses all its tension goes slack.

Attachment geometry

We started with two Y-frames and a floating tristar, drawing on the tensegrity leg design by Mortensen and colleagues. Each of the three tristar tips connects to all four frame tips, giving us 12 support spans. The tristar arms are 120° apart, and the frame tips sit on the intended flexion axis. Read the study.

We used 60° of knee flexion as a common reference for the STEP assembly and the force model. Moving one anchor changes the pull direction of several tendons. Using the same pose let us see how the attachment geometry redistributed load.

How the tendons share load

In one static sizing case, we applied an assumed 3.2 kN horizontal load and 5.25 kN vertical load. At the same tristar tip, P1, the model gave about 5.69 kN in each span to the upper frame and 0.50 kN in each span to the lower frame.

That difference is why we checked individual spans. The lightest span tells us which is closest to going slack, and the heaviest tells us which connection carries the most load. Adding pretension gives the lightest span more room before it goes slack and also raises the load at the anchors.

Modelling everyday loads

We began with a mass, spring, and damper on a fixed support. The spring responds to displacement, the damper responds to speed, and the two forces add at the support. We ran 20 cycles each of walking, running, and squatting to see how much travel and force the model predicted.

We then let the lower support rotate to represent motion at the ankle. That changed what the spring and damper felt because both of their ends could now move. Running produced the highest transmitted force in both models. Its peak went from 4.28 kN with the fixed base to 4.33 kN with the rotating support, and the mass’s peak displacement rose from about 198 to 207 mm.

Both reference models use constant stiffness and damping. The runs compare load cases and support conditions. The relationship between tendon pretension, knee angle, and the assembled joint’s stiffness remains open.

Stress in the rigid parts

We checked the upper frame, lower frame, and tristar separately to see where stress builds up. The maps show a representative setup with a fixed region and a force applied to one arm. In the separate activity cases, the lower frame, or tibial stem, had the highest reported stress and displacement of the three parts.

The squat case gave the largest stress in the tibial stem, while running gave the largest transmitted force in the separate Simulink model. Those results helped us distinguish the load on the support from the stress within an individual part.

Exploring the Fireworks Galaxy

School project · CFAL Astronomy Club

I started this project at CFAL Astronomy Club to look at the bright centre of the Fireworks Galaxy, NGC 6946. I used one near-infrared Hubble exposure and worked from the full detector frame down to the nucleus. I later restored the notebook and rechecked the data quality and display settings.

Literature and image selection

I used the Black Eye Galaxy, NGC 4826, for the literature survey. Its dark dust band crosses one side of a bright centre, which raised a question about how the band looks at different wavelengths. For the notebook analysis I used the Fireworks Galaxy from the same NICMOS survey. Hubble’s Black Eye Galaxy overview · NICMOS survey.

Calibrated exposure and pixel mask

I used a 256 × 256 F160W exposure from 19 January 1998. The science plane gives count rates, and the data-quality plane flags unreliable pixels. After masking flagged data, 65,045 of 65,536 pixels remained. Their median was 1.96 counts/s, and their 98th percentile was 11.24 counts/s. Original FITS exposure from MAST.

Some flagged pixels sit beside the bright centre. Checking the mask first gave me a better basis for choosing a display stretch that also showed the fainter field.

Nucleus crops

I made three progressively tighter crops. The wide view located the bright patch, and the tightest crop showed its shape. In the original plot each panel had its own colour scaling. In the restored notebook I applied a shared stretch and marked flagged pixels, which made it easier to compare the same feature across crops.

I put both versions beside each other because independent colour scaling can make a feature look stronger simply because the crop changed. The shared scale lets me compare brightness across views, and the mask tells me where the image cannot support that comparison.

Position and radial profile

I mapped the catalogued infrared position onto the detector before drawing the cutouts. The brightest usable pixel was about 0.41 arcseconds from that position. Before filtering, the median count rate was 55.58 counts/s within five pixels of the centre and 4.15 counts/s in the ring from 30 to 60 pixels. Measuring the calibrated data in rings gave me a way to describe how quickly the light falls off. SIMBAD position for NGC 6946.

Sato and Meijering filters

I first ran Sato and Meijering on each crop with both set to detect dark ridges. The apparent shapes around the bright patch changed with the filter. I checked them against the calibrated image and compared both filters in bright-ridge mode as well.

Filter comparison

I ran both filters on the full frame and took the same 96-pixel cutout from each result. Sato gives a broader response around the centre, while Meijering concentrates the strongest response more tightly. Within each row I used the same scaling across the five colour palettes so I could see what the palette alone changed.

The central brightening is already visible in the calibrated exposure. The filters helped me see how the apparent shape depends on the method, and the radial profile gave me a separate measure of the light distribution in that infrared band.

Filtering before cropping also gave each method the same surrounding pixels. That matters near a crop edge, where the filter has less neighbouring data to work with. I used the response images to compare what the filters emphasised, and used the radial profile for the actual count rates.

Run the notebook

The restored notebook includes the literature survey, data checks, and code for all four figures. It retrieves the original calibrated exposure from the public Hubble archive. Open in Google Colab · Download the notebook.

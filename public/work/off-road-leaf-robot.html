Terrain-Adaptive Leaf Cleaning Module

NITK · Virtual Instrumentation team project · Jul 2026 — Present

We are developing a cleaning module that mounts beneath a collection hopper. The goal is to pick up wet and dry leaves on uneven ground while limiting how much soil enters the collector.

We completed the six-unit CAD assembly and demonstrated a printed tiller unit outdoors.

Why make a separate cleaning module?

Wet leaves can sit close to the ground, especially in low spots on an uneven surface. A pickup set at a fixed height can pass over those leaves or dig into higher patches and bring in soil. We wanted the part that meets the ground to accommodate those height changes.

We focused on the cleaning module below the hopper. The mounting plate is the interface to the host platform, and the tiller units handle the contact with the surface across the pickup’s width.

Keeping the pickup close to the ground

The mounting plate carries six identical tiller units. Each has a two-stage telescoping shaft, a guide collar, and a roller held in a yoke. The roller makes contact with the ground while the shaft can extend and retract vertically. This lets the pickup accommodate changes in surface height across its width.

Splitting the width into six units let us study the motion of one shaft and roller first. In the assembly view, I could then see how those units line up under the mounting plate and how much space each has around its neighbours.

Designing for fabrication

We repeated one tiller design to reduce the number of unique parts. The design review identified the structural parts for PETG printing and the bought hardware, including the rollers, springs, and rack-and-pinion components. We used drawings of the single unit and the full assembly to make that breakdown clear.

The bill of materials also helped separate what had to be made to fit the module from parts that could be bought. That was useful when reviewing a design that repeats the same set of parts across its width.

Motion study and outdoor demonstration

In CAD, we followed the shaft through extension and retraction and checked where the roller reaches the ground line. We then demonstrated a printed tiller unit outdoors. The motion study shows the designed travel, and the demonstration shows one printed unit operating in a field setting.

What I learned

I learned to distinguish the behaviour of one repeated unit from what happens when several units sit under the same plate. The single-unit study made the lift motion visible, and the array assembly exposed the spacing and mounting questions that were easy to miss in isolation.

The outdoor demonstration also gave me a clearer distinction between demonstrating a mechanism and measuring cleaning performance. Leaf pickup, soil intake, and repeatability describe different parts of how a cleaning module performs.

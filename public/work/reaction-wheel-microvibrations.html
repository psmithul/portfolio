Physics-Informed Multi-Fidelity FEM Surrogate for Reaction-Wheel-Induced Spacecraft Microvibration and Payload Jitter

Computational study · 5–20 Hz reduced-model pilot · Aug — Oct 2026

I built a faster model of how vibration from four reaction wheels reaches a spacecraft camera. I used it to test whether a stiffer mount would improve the simulated pointing response over 5–20 Hz.

Computational pilot over 5–20 Hz. Neural training uses reduced-model labels, with selected full-FEM points used for a separate numerical comparison.

Why study the pointing response?

I wanted to see how a disturbance at a reaction wheel shows up at a camera elsewhere on a spacecraft. The camera support can tilt as the structure flexes, even when the overall spacecraft motion has been accounted for. That makes the path through the mount and the rest of the structure part of the pointing problem.

The other question was whether I could compare many changes to the mount without solving the full finite-element model for each one. That gave the reduced model and the neural models a specific job: reproduce the structural response closely enough to screen mount settings.

Following the vibration to the camera

I started with a free-floating spacecraft model in COMSOL. At each of four reaction wheels, I applied two transverse force inputs and two moment inputs, giving 16 inputs in all. For camera tilt, I compared the average motion at two stations on the camera support about 0.48 m apart and removed the spacecraft’s overall rotation. The resulting angle is a structural estimate of pointing motion.

A smaller model for repeated calculations

The original mesh had about 1.01 million volume elements. To run many configurations over 5–20 Hz, I built a smaller model using 36 natural modes. I added 16 corrections for bending around the camera support that the first version missed. Those 52 coordinates became the reference for training and comparing the neural models.

The enrichment comparison was how I checked what the smaller basis was leaving out. A set of modes can describe the large-scale motion well while missing local bending at the camera support. The added corrections mattered because the output I cared about was measured at that support.

Comparing MLP, DeepONet, and physics-informed models

I kept whole structural configurations together when splitting the data. A model that has seen one part of a frequency curve has already seen the structure behind the rest of the curve.

The multilayer perceptron (MLP) came closest to the 52-coordinate reference in the original comparison. The table reports mean frequency-integrated complex-response errors on three unseen configurations, with all 16 inputs tested on each.

Response MLP DeepONet
Camera displacement 0.139% 0.342%
Optical bench displacement 0.171% 0.378%
Structural tilt 0.261% 0.701%

Trying a different way to train

Starting with 36-coordinate training data helped when I had few 52-coordinate cases. The benefit went away with the largest training set. In a separate single-input experiment, adding an equilibrium term to the training loss lowered the balanced residual by 9.23%. The resonance results were mixed.

Why I kept the original mount

I screened 17 mount stiffness settings. The neural model picked a 10% increase, so I checked that setting and the original against the 52-coordinate model on 36 separate scenarios. The worst response in that set changed by just 0.00589%, while the largest neural peak error was 7.61%. The difference was too small to justify changing the mount.

Checks against the full COMSOL model

I compared the predictions with 14 full-model points across six configurations. Four of those points came from configurations held out of the original comparison. In a separate wheel-speed study, changing the mesh shifted the peak amplitude near 5,800 rpm by 0.33%.

The 52-coordinate reference was what I used for training and most comparisons. The selected full COMSOL cases provided a separate numerical check. The tilt shown here is a modeled structural response, with the comparisons limited to the reported cases.

What I learned

Checking the suggested mount change against the reduced model taught me to compare the size of a design benefit with the error in the tool that suggested it. A low average response error helped the model screen options. The peak error mattered more when the two candidate settings were almost tied.

The training comparison also helped me see why it matters to hold out whole configurations. Keeping individual frequency points separate would still let a model learn the shape of the same structure from nearby points. The grouped split made the comparison closer to the way I was using the model.

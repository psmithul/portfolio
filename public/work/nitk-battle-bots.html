Combat Robot Development

Team mechanical design · NITK Battle Bots

At NITK Battle Bots, we designed a 3 lb flipper with a one-piece chassis and built the 15 kg GN4RLY 2.5. The two builds taught me how packaging, fabrication, and repair access affect a team at an event.

We assembled GN4RLY 2.5 in the Robocon Lab and competed with it at the event shown below.

Where we started

The two weight classes gave us different design problems. On the 3 lb build, we were trying to make a small chassis with fewer separate parts. The 15 kg build gave us more room to work with, and assembly meant bringing larger mechanical and electrical parts together within a fixed envelope and budget.

The event gave those decisions a practical consequence. We had to get the robot assembled and ready with the tools, time, and money the team had.

3 lb flipper

For the 3 lb class, we designed the chassis and flipper together. We liked the rear-hinged lifter on Jelly Baby and used it as a starting point. At that weight, every separate part was another piece we had to make and fit, so we went with a one-piece chassis. That kept the build simpler and cheaper for the team. Jelly Baby’s NHRL profile.

The two build photos show why we checked the shape with the components installed. The chassis looks simple on its own. Once the battery and electronics are in place, the remaining room for wiring and assembly is much easier to judge.

GN4RLY 2.5 (15 kg)

Sponsorship gave us room to use stronger materials on GN4RLY 2.5. The workbench photo shows what had to fit in the frame. The hammer saw sits in the centre, the chain drives run along the sides, and the wiring is still exposed during assembly.

Why GN4RLY 2.5 fell short

We carried over a hammer design we had made two years earlier. At the event, the weight of the robot and the older assembly held us back, and we couldn’t advance to later rounds. We needed a redesign, but the money left after the build wouldn’t cover fabrication.

The workshop build put the layout in front of us at full scale. The frame had to leave space for the drivetrain and electronics, with parts fitted in an order the team could actually assemble. Seeing everything on the bench made those constraints clearer than the drawings alone.

What I learned

I learned different things from the workbench and from the event. Assembling the small chassis made the value of fewer parts obvious. At the event, GN4RLY 2.5 showed us the cost of carrying over an older design when the weight budget, packaging, and repair constraints had changed.

Working with the team also made me think about cost at the assembly level. An available part can save money on its own while making the complete build harder to fit, work on, or replace.

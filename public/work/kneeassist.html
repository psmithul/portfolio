Actuated Knee-Assistance System

Passive concept to actuated brace · Team project · Oct 2025 — Sep 2026

We developed a passive spring mechanism into a brace that uses a motor and cable to assist knee extension. Our team placed in the top five at the Incubate X Prosthetic Challenge.

We developed the mechanism in CAD and defined the drive, sensing, power, and manual-release architecture.

What prompted the design

We started with the idea of using a spring to help someone straighten their knee. As we worked through the mechanism, it became clear that the amount of help changes with the joint angle. The spring stretches by a different amount and pulls at a different angle as the knee bends.

That led us to look at both the geometry of the mechanism and how to control the assistance. The passive version gave us a way to understand the spring and its leverage before we added a drive.

From a spring to a motor-driven brace

We started with a passive spring-assisted knee mechanism. We moved the spring attachment points in SolidWorks and ANSYS to see what happened as the knee bent. Two things mattered in each position. The spring needed enough leverage to assist the knee, and the parts needed room to move through the full range.

The later design adds a motor-driven cable to load the spring and a sensor to track the knee angle. The thigh and shank cuffs sit on adjustable rails, with the drive and spring on one side. The user still extends their knee actively, and the cable and spring add assistance.

I used the changing poses in SolidWorks and ANSYS to look for interference around the pivot, spring, and attachments. The work separated two decisions that are easy to mix together: where the linkage needs to move and where the cable needs to pull to get useful leverage.

How the brace helps the knee extend

As the spring stretches, it pulls harder. The angle of the cable also changes as the knee moves, which changes its leverage about the pivot. That’s why the spring force alone couldn’t tell us how much assistance the brace would provide throughout the motion.

We also designed an independent manual release. If the drive jams, the user needs a way to release the mechanism without moving the motor.

In the actuated design, the angle sensor gives the drive a measure of the brace position. The motor changes the load on the spring through the cable. We treated the cuffs and rails as part of the mechanism because their position determines how the device sits relative to the knee.

What we completed

We took the design from the passive spring concept to an actuated CAD assembly and defined how the drive, sensing, power, and release fit together. The team presented that work at the Incubate X Prosthetic Challenge and placed in the top 5 of 70 teams nationwide.

What I learned

The sequence of working on the passive design first helped me understand what the motor actually controls. Spring force and extension assistance are related through the cable’s changing leverage. Looking at the same mechanism at several angles made that relationship easier to see.

Working through the jam case taught me to account for how someone releases a mechanism as part of the design. The manual release had to have its own path because the drive might be the part that had stopped moving.

Variable-Stiffness Suspension and Vibration-Aware Control for a Rough-Terrain Rover

Mechanical design and control development · Sep 2026 — Present

We designed a six-wheel rover for inspection routes where rough ground shakes the camera and sensors. We wanted to see whether changing the suspension response could keep the chassis steadier at the same driving speed.

We developed the wheel module and full rover in CAD and defined how the vibration and preload controls work together.

Suspension concept

Our first concept coupled two suspension units. A cable guide moved between three positions to change the leverage of the spring. We sketched how to lock the chosen setting before the rover moved again.

In the later CAD, we gave each wheel its own motor and preload carriage. Moving the adjustment to the wheel meant packaging a screw, guides, and a lock in every module. We kept the lock so the suspension could work passively after an adjustment.

The cable-guide sketch changed the leverage of a coupled pair. In the individual-wheel version, the motor changes the initial tension in the elastic paths on that wheel. That gives each module its own setting and keeps the adjustment hardware within the wheel assembly.

Wheel module design

As the wheel rises, the carrier pivots, three compression links change angle, and three elastic paths stretch. Those elastic paths pull the carrier back. A separate damper helps the wheel settle after a bump.

A geared motor turns a fine-pitch screw. Guide rails prevent the carriage from rotating, so it slides along the screw and changes the starting tension in the elastic paths. The CAD provides 6 mm of carriage travel. Once the carriage is locked, the carrier can still pivot as the wheel follows the ground.

I separated the part that sets preload from the part that moves over a bump. With the carriage held in place, the wheel can respond to the ground without the motor having to turn at every bump.

Wheel travel in CAD

We posed the module at neutral and at +20° of bump. In the model, the wheel centre rises about 16 mm between those poses. From −8° of droop to +20° of bump, the model gives about 21 mm of vertical travel.

Comparing the poses made the carrier’s path clear. The wheel centre follows an arc, so vertical travel is measured from the change in its height. These are geometric values from the assembly.

I read wheel travel and adjuster travel from different parts of the model. The roughly 21 mm figure is the vertical range of the wheel centre. The 6 mm figure is the movement of the preload carriage. That check establishes the motion envelope in CAD. The force response under load is a separate question.

Chassis layout

We placed six copies of the module around the central deck. The roof-off model shows their positions relative to the electronics space. The closed model shows where the body sits relative to the wheel assemblies.

Sensing and preload control

We put accelerometers on the middle wheel carriers and the chassis in the sensor layout. Comparing those locations gives us a way to relate what the wheels encounter to the motion that reaches the body. Wheel encoders measure speed, and the chassis IMU tracks roll and pitch.

In the control design, sustained chassis vibration triggers a pause and a preload adjustment. The logic checks the carriage position and lock before resuming. Speed reduction is a separate response to sustained vibration.

The CAD established the travel and packaging of the module. Its loaded force response depends on the elastic paths, damping, and contact with the ground.

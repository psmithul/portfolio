Wall-Climbing Robot

ISTE NITK · CLUTCH team project · October 2024 — March 2025

We built a small robot that climbed concrete walls and streamed video for crack inspection. A ducted fan pressed the four driven wheels against the wall, and a camera sent back footage that we processed to find cracks.

Keeping grip on the wall

We modelled the chassis in CAD around the ducted fan, four wheel motors, and camera. The fan pushes the robot towards the wall. That increases the normal force on the tyres, giving them more grip to support the robot’s weight and drive it along the surface.

We used the robot’s weight and assumed friction coefficients for different surfaces to estimate the minimum thrust needed to keep the wheels from slipping.

The reconstructed design uses a 3D-printed frame with ribs around the motor mounts and small trays for the battery and control boards. Mounting the fan on its outer rim leaves the middle of the frame open. Keeping the chassis light also reduces the thrust the wheels need for grip.

Fan clearance in ANSYS

We ran an airflow study in ANSYS to compare thrust at different distances between the wall and the fan inlet. Air enters around the sides of the gap, turns into the duct, and leaves through the exhaust. The fan pushes air away from the wall, and the reaction force presses the robot towards it.

With more clearance, air has more room to turn into the inlet. A very tight gap can restrict how much air reaches the fan at a given speed, reducing thrust and leaving less grip at the wheels. On uneven concrete, a raised patch can narrow that gap as the robot climbs. Related inlet clearance experiment.

Driving the robot

A LiPo battery supplied the wheel drivers and the fan’s electronic speed controller (ESC). A regulated 5 V supply fed the control electronics. The wireless receiver sent steering and drive commands to the Arduino, which controlled the four DC wheel motors through their drivers. A separate radio channel set the fan speed through the brushless ESC.

Looking for cracks

We mounted a wireless camera on the chassis and streamed video of the concrete surface as the robot moved. We processed the frames using a percolation method to find connected regions that followed the shape of a crack.

UAV Vibration Analysis & Isolation

Product Intern · Vayu Aerospace Pvt. Ltd., Bengaluru · 1 June – 15 July 2026

I compared three ways to mount a UAV flight controller at Vayu Aerospace. Ground motor tests helped us choose an elastomer-isolated tray.

The goal was to reduce the motor vibration reaching the flight controller’s inertial measurement unit (IMU) while keeping the electronics accessible.

Original project images and measured vibration values are redacted under a nondisclosure agreement (NDA). The 3D renders illustrate the mounting concepts.

Tracing the vibration path

I started by getting familiar with the airframe, flight controller, and onboard logs. The controller uses accelerometer and gyroscope readings from its inertial measurement unit (IMU) to estimate how the aircraft is moving. When vibration travels from the motors through the airframe and into the controller mount, it also appears in those readings.

I focused on the mechanical path between the airframe and the controller tray. The mount had to reduce the vibration reaching the sensor while holding the controller in a repeatable orientation and leaving room to connect and service the electronics.

Comparing the mounts in ANSYS

I modeled the tray, its supports, and the frame-side attachments in ANSYS Mechanical. I kept the electronics mass and its location comparable across concepts and represented fastened joints and compliant supports separately. Treating a flexible support as a rigid bond would remove the isolation path I was trying to compare.

The modal analysis showed where each mount could resonate and how the tray moved in those modes, including whether it translated or rocked. I then compared how excitation on the airframe side reached the tray. The rigid mount provided a baseline. The elastomer tray added compliance and damping in a compact package, while the suspended mount allowed more relative motion and made clearance and rocking more important.

Fitting the mounts into the aircraft

We shortlisted the elastomer and suspended concepts for hardware testing. I checked the manufactured parts against the installation space, including fastener fit and access to the connectors. I kept the controller orientation consistent across the assemblies so the same IMU axes could be compared.

I also checked that the tray had room to move and that the wiring had enough slack. A taut cable or contact with nearby structure can create a parallel path for vibration, so these checks were part of verifying that the assembled mount behaved like the design we had analyzed.

Ground-test results

We ran the motors with the aircraft on the ground and logged the controller’s IMU for the rigid baseline and both isolation mounts. In MATLAB, I reviewed the time histories and selected comparable steady portions of each run so startup and other transients would not skew the comparison.

I compared root-mean-square (RMS) vibration by axis to see how much overall motion reached the controller in each configuration. I then used the fast Fourier transform (FFT) to compare the dominant peaks in the spectra. The two views let me distinguish changes in overall vibration from changes concentrated at particular frequencies.

Both isolation mounts reduced measured IMU vibration relative to the rigid baseline. The ANSYS models helped explain how the mounts could move, and the IMU logs showed what reached the sensor in the assembled hardware.

The actual vibration measurements are confidential under the NDA.

Why we selected the elastomer tray

The team selected the elastomer-isolated modular tray after weighing the ground-test results and ANSYS analysis alongside fit, wiring, and assembly. The tray kept the electronics together in a compact package and was easier to install consistently than the more freely suspended arrangement.

The report recommends repeating the ground runs at several motor operating points and adding a second accelerometer on the frame. Measuring the frame and controller simultaneously would show how much of the input vibration passes through the mount. Repeated runs would show how much that response varies before moving to flight validation.

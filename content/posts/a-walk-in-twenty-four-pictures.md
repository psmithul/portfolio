A man walks across a page carrying a bag and a cane. He does it again, a fraction of a second further along. Then again. His journey is small enough to hold in your hands, but it takes several rows to complete.

In Eadweard Muybridge’s _Animal Locomotion, Plate 49_, the familiar action has been divided into photographs. You can linger at a point that would usually be gone before you thought to notice it.

![Four rows of sequential photographs of a suited man walking with a cane and a bag.](/images/muybridge-plate-49.jpg)

There is something slightly comic about such elaborate attention to a walk. All those pictures, for something we do on the way to something else. Then you try to describe precisely what happens between one foot leaving the ground and the other taking the weight, and the comedy gives way to respect.

Ordinary is a measure of how often we encounter a thing. It says little about how simple the thing is.

## Between the frames

The plate offers more information than a glance, and it also makes its omissions visible. These are positions. The movement between them has to be reconstructed in the imagination. The photographs cannot tell you how heavy the bag feels, or whether the man is tired, or where he wants to go.

That boundary interests me. A useful description earns its usefulness by leaving things out. A drawing of a hinge need not describe the room around it. A route on a map need not contain the sound of the road. If every description included everything, it would become another world to navigate.

The trouble begins when we forget what we chose to leave out.

A beautifully clear model can make its subject seem more completely understood than it is. Lines and labels have an authority that untidy reality rarely gets to enjoy. The missing information does not announce itself. You have to remember to ask for it.

Muybridge’s gaps are at least honest. They are right there, between the pictures.

## The patience of a second look

There is another reason to spend time with a plate like this. It slows down the urge to explain.

It is tempting to move directly from noticing something to having a theory about it. An explanation feels productive. You have turned an observation into a piece of knowledge. But a quick explanation also gives the eye permission to stop working.

Looking longer can make the question better. Why that position of the hand? What changes when the body turns? Which details remain similar from one view to another? You do not have to answer all of them for the looking to have been worthwhile.

I find this a more appealing account of curiosity than the demand to be fascinated by everything. Curiosity can be quite local. One page. One movement. One detail that does not behave as expected.

The man on the plate never gets beyond his allotted rectangle. Even so, there is enough in his short walk to keep the eye busy. He has somewhere to go. For a little while, we do not.

Most of the photograph is somewhere we cannot live.

There is the grey edge of the Moon, with its battered surface. Above it, a darkness so even that the eye has very little to do. Then, almost awkwardly small, the Earth. A little weather. A bright curve. Everything familiar, compressed into a part of the picture you could cover with your thumb.

![Blue-and-white Earth above the grey lunar horizon, surrounded by the blackness of space.](/images/earthrise.jpg)

_Earthrise. Bill Anders / NASA, Apollo 8, 24 December 1968. [NASA’s account of the photograph](https://science.nasa.gov/resource/apollo-8s-iconic-earthrise/)._

The picture has become so familiar that it is possible to recognise it without really looking at it. Earthrise: space exploration, environmental awareness, our shared home. We have a ready-made drawer for it. Into the drawer it goes.

But recognition can be a surprisingly effective way of avoiding attention.

The details still have something to say. The Earth is not centred. The Moon occupies a great deal of space without offering much comfort. There is no border around the inhabited part of the universe, no typographic instruction telling us where to look. The photograph trusts us to find the small thing that matters.

## What scale leaves out

A change of scale does not necessarily bring an increase in understanding. From far enough away, a city becomes a patch of colour. You can see its shape more clearly and know almost nothing about the lives within it. Move close enough to a single window and the city disappears.

Both views are useful. Neither can do the other’s work.

This seems easy to remember when looking at a photograph. It becomes harder with a difficult day. A small disappointment can occupy the entire frame. The mind crops out the rest with impressive efficiency: the ordinary conversation that went well, the problem that finally made sense, the fact that tomorrow remains available.

I don’t think the answer is to tell yourself that your troubles are insignificant because the universe is large. A headache is not relieved by the diameter of Jupiter. Scale can offer some room around a feeling without ruling on whether the feeling deserves to exist.

Perhaps that is the useful part of Earthrise. It does not erase the foreground. It lets another thing enter the frame.

## Looking twice

There is a small practice hidden here: stay with something for a moment after you have identified it.

A tree, after “tree.” A person, after their job title. A piece of work, after deciding whether it succeeded. What else is present? What did the name allow you to stop seeing?

This need not turn every walk into an exercise in self-improvement. Some days a tree is simply something providing shade. Still, I like the idea that the familiar world has not necessarily exhausted itself. Perhaps we have only become quick at filing it away.

Earthrise is over half a century old. Its subject is much older. The photograph has not changed when it catches someone’s attention again. The available change is on our side of the glass.

That small blue thing has been there all along.

There are two kinds of satisfaction in a notebook. One comes from a page that explains something clearly. The other comes from a page that finally admits what is unclear.

The first is easier to show someone.

It has a title, perhaps a tidy diagram. The arrows know where they are going. The second page has a calculation abandoned halfway down, a word circled twice, and a question whose answer may invalidate the previous three pages. It looks like less has been accomplished. Sometimes more has.

I like a finished piece of work. I like the small relief of being able to put it down. But finishing can become a habit of presentation before it becomes a fact about understanding. You can make a page look settled while the question underneath it is still moving.

## A place for “not yet”

An unfinished thought is uncomfortable partly because it gives you no clean account of your progress. You have spent an afternoon with a problem. What can you say you have done? “The first approach does not work, and I’m beginning to suspect the question is slightly wrong” is an awkward answer.

It may also be an accurate and useful one.

There is a difference between avoiding a decision and making space for the evidence that would improve it. The two can look alike from outside. They can even feel alike from inside, which is inconvenient. A question needs time, but it can also become a very comfortable place to hide.

One way to distinguish them is to give the uncertainty a specific task. What would change your mind? What could you draw, read, measure, or build next? A vague doubt can expand to fill any available space. A doubt attached to a check has somewhere to go.

That is why a rough note can be more useful than a polished conclusion. “Check what happens at the boundary” offers the next person, including your future self, a way back into the work.

## Keeping the rough page

We rarely see the whole route behind something finished. A paper presents an argument in an order chosen to make it understandable. A mechanism arrives with its failed versions removed. An essay leaves out the paragraph that briefly seemed indispensable and then, with a night’s sleep, did not.

This is usually a kindness to the reader. Nobody owes us their entire wastebasket.

Still, it is easy to compare the inside of your own work with the outside of someone else’s. Your crossed-out sentences meet their published page. Your uncertain first model meets their final drawing. The comparison has been arranged to make you lose.

Keeping a few rough pages is a modest correction. They record that confusion was part of the work, and sometimes the part that moved it forward. They also make a useful distinction visible: being unable to explain something today does not mean you will be unable to explain it next week.

There should be a place in a notebook where a question can remain a question. No inspirational heading required. Just enough room to return, make another mark, and see what happens.
